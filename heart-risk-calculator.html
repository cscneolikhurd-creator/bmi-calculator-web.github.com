Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good heart risk score?

Per ACC/AHA: Low risk is below 5%, borderline is 5-7.4%, intermediate is 7.5-19.9%, and high risk is 20%+. High risk typically warrants statin therapy and aggressive lifestyle changes.

How is heart age calculated?

Heart age compares your cardiovascular risk to the average risk for someone of your age. If your 10-year risk equals a 65-year-old's risk, your heart age is 65 — even if you're 50. It's a powerful motivational tool.

What is the difference between Framingham and ASCVD?

Framingham (1998) is an older, well-validated model derived from the Framingham Heart Study. ASCVD Pooled Cohort Equations (2013) are the current ACC/AHA standard for US adults, and include African American-specific coefficients.

Why do South Asians have higher heart risk?

South Asians have 2-4x higher cardiovascular risk compared to White populations, even at the same BMI. Contributing factors: higher visceral fat, lower HDL, higher triglycerides, insulin resistance, and genetic predisposition. Guidelines recommend earlier screening and more aggressive targets.

Can I lower my heart risk score?

Yes. Key steps: quit smoking (cuts risk ~50% in 1 year), control blood pressure (target <130/80), lower LDL cholesterol (statins if needed), manage diabetes, exercise 150+ min/week, eat a Mediterranean/DASH diet, maintain healthy weight, reduce stress.

Is this calculator accurate?

This tool uses simplified but validated approximations of the Framingham and ASCVD algorithms. For clinical decision-making (like starting statins), your doctor will use the full equations with additional factors like family history and LDL cholesterol. Use this tool for education and motivation.

Medical Disclaimer

This heart risk calculator is for educational and motivational purposes only. Risk scores are estimates based on population averages and simplified algorithms — they do not replace clinical risk assessment using the full Framingham or ASCVD equations.

This tool is not a substitute for professional medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Do not start, stop, or change medications (including statins, antihypertensives, or beta-blockers) based on these results. Consult your cardiologist or primary care physician for personalized risk assessment and treatment decisions.

If you experience chest pain, shortness of breath, fainting, or other cardiac symptoms, call emergency services immediately.
